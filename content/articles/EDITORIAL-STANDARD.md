# HOME editorial standard

This file defines the mandatory editorial standard for HOME articles in Spanish and English.

## The reader comes before the terminology

Every article must be understandable by a normal homeowner without specialist knowledge. Technical accuracy is required, but technical vocabulary must never be used as a shortcut for explanation.

- Use the everyday expression first.
- If a technical term is useful, introduce it immediately after the plain-language explanation.
- If a reader would reasonably need to search a word in order to understand our answer, the explanation is not finished.
- Never assume knowledge of construction, plumbing, HVAC, appliance-repair, pest-control or regulatory vocabulary.

Examples:

- Prefer `el espacio bajo el suelo de algunas viviendas (cámara sanitaria)` to an unexplained `cámara sanitaria`.
- Prefer `el serpentín interior, la parte fría por la que pasa el aire` to an unexplained `batería evaporadora`.
- Prefer `el agua de condensación` to `los condensados` unless the technical term adds something useful.

## Acronyms and institutions

Acronyms must be expanded the first time they appear in article prose. The reader must know what the organization is and, when relevant, what country it belongs to.

Spanish examples:

- `Agencia de Protección Ambiental de EE. UU. (EPA)`
- `Centros para el Control y la Prevención de Enfermedades de EE. UU. (CDC)`
- `Departamento de Agricultura de EE. UU. (USDA)`
- `Comisión de Seguridad de Productos del Consumidor de EE. UU. (CPSC)`

After the first explanation, the acronym may be used when it improves readability.

Institutions support the answer; they must not become the narrator. Prefer explaining the practical rule first and attributing it naturally when attribution adds confidence. The structured `sources` array remains the canonical source list.

## Localization is editorial, not literal translation

Spanish and English versions share search intent, article number and translation group, but each version must read as if it was written for its own market.

Spanish articles:

- Prefer metric units first. Add U.S. units in parentheses when they are useful for manuals, product labels or U.S. sources.
- Translate interface concepts before showing an English label: `Cancelar/Desaguar (Cancel/Drain, si así aparece en el panel)`.
- Explain market-specific concepts such as `air gap`, `tankless`, `PRV`, `TDS`, `HE` or `MERV` before expecting the reader to recognize them.
- A U.S. source is valid evidence, but its country context must be clear when it matters.

English articles should use natural U.S./Canadian household vocabulary and may retain familiar local terms, while still expanding institutional acronyms on first use when the meaning is not obvious from context.

## Human narrative, not a visible template

The article needs a thread of reasoning, but the reader should not be able to see the writing template.

- Start from the real situation that brought the reader to the page.
- Give the core answer early.
- Develop the diagnosis or method in the order a person would actually use it.
- Explain why each important step matters.
- Use headings to help reading, not to manufacture length.
- Vary transitions and endings. Do not repeatedly end articles with headings such as `La secuencia que...`, `La regla que...` or `La pista clave...` just because previous articles did.
- Avoid repeatedly using the construction `la pregunta no es X, sino Y` across the library.
- Tables are for comparison or scanning; prose must still interpret what the table means.

## No editorial metadiscourse

The published article must never discuss the editorial process or tell the reader what HOME as a publication chooses to do.

Avoid phrases such as:

- `HOME prioriza...`
- `HOME no plantea estos artículos...`
- `En este artículo hemos decidido...`

Express the recommendation directly and let the editorial philosophy be visible through the quality of the advice.

## Safety without robotic language

Safety boundaries must be clear, specific and calm. Explain why the boundary exists when that helps the reader make a decision. Do not hide a useful answer behind generic warnings, but do not encourage work that requires qualifications, hazardous chemical mixing, live electrical testing, refrigerant handling or unsafe structural access.

## Final editorial tests

Before publishing, every article must pass these checks:

1. **Cold-reader test:** can a non-specialist understand every paragraph without looking up unexplained jargon?
2. **Acronym test:** is every non-obvious acronym explained on first use?
3. **Localization test:** do units, appliance labels, agencies and terminology fit the language/market rather than look translated?
4. **Heading-removal test:** if the headings vanished, would the prose still form a coherent explanation rather than a stack of answers?
5. **Human-cadence test:** do sentence rhythm, transitions and the ending feel specific to this topic rather than copied from a template?
6. **No-metadiscourse test:** does the article speak only to the reader's problem, never about HOME's editorial process?
7. **Second-search test:** after reading, should the user reasonably be able to act or make the next decision without another obvious search?

There is no fixed word count. The article is long enough when the search intent is fully resolved and short enough when additional text would only repeat the same idea.
